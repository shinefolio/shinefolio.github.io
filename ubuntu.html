UBUNTU

Spring 2025
Solo Project/Designer
Genre: Slice-Of-Life

Synopsis: 
Ubuntu is a 2D top-down simulation game. You play as a humanoid character traversing across a somber, metaphysical ocean. Use items to restore the environment. Meet various player stand-ins offering you a choice to take or double a seemingly random quantity of consumables. Discover the hidden secrets of the world.

GAME DESIGN DOCUMENT

a multiplayer interactive narrative experience

In 2022, the Take It Or Double It And Give It To The Next Person social experiment was propagated by social media. Today, it is an often parodied trend which allows for an experiment in behavioral economics regarding the threshold in which individuals would chose to prolong the doubling chain.

This was a solo project to design a game that tested the human capacity for solidarity in a private setting.
Credits / mohdihsanabdulrauf

emotion by design
Ubuntu was designed to foster a sense of solidarity among players by using game mechanics that emphasise indirect interaction between players, highlighting the impact of communal effort.

The core loop consisting of the Take It or Double It mechanic places the player in a sacrificial dilemma of whether to take a risk and Double It for the next player to stand a better chance of survival, or Take It for themselves as a means of temporary self-preservation.

This, along with other supporting game mechanics, enabled players to exist in a shared world where solidarity and cooperation were mutually beneficial as a means to fulfil the goal of the game.

the challenge

The goal of this project was to design an innovative game system that utilised the principles of Multiplayer Interactive Narrative Experiences (MINEs) to create impactful narratives. MINEs are characterised by a narrative system known as player differentiability in which each player experiences a distinct narrative. This is typically a result of player interactions affecting both their own storylines and that of other players. ​

HIGH LEVEL GOALS

1. Create a distinct, replayable game system utilising the principles of MINEs
2. Utilise environmental storytelling to deliver a narrative experience without the need for dialogue
3. Leverage narrative techniques to deliver an impactful narrative twist

MY ROLE

​Ubuntu started development in Unity during the spring of 2025, concluding after about a month. As it was a solo project, I was responsible for design, systems, narrative, programming, sound and art assets. Third party assets were sourced for sound and additional art. Supplementary programming assistance was obtained.

project kickoff

Research was conducted on existing MINEs and the game's target audience. This research provided insights into the niche market of MINEs and informed the development of player archetypes and anti-personas, helping to articulate the motivations behind different player behaviours. I discovered that many existing MINEs, despite presenting as single-player experiences, often have players share the same story instance and reading state. A notable example is Dark Souls, where players can trigger interactions that manifest in the game worlds of others. This principle was applied to Ubuntu as a basic building block of system construction.

game mechanics

Restoration mechanic showcase
REPLAYABILITY AS A MECHANIC
Multiple playthroughs where players chose to Double It are required to gain a meaningful number of consumables for extended gameplay, encouraging players to replay the game to increase the number of consumables in circulation.
RESTORATION AS A COMMUNITY-DRIVEN GOAL
Players restore a sparse, empty world to one filled with the lush vegetation of life. Areas restored by the playthrough of previous players can appear within the playthroughs of new players. By observing the positive effects of this restoration process in populating the previously monochromatic rocky environment, players are encouraged to cooperate in a prosocial manner to restore the game’s world at large.

a step into the metaphysical

Ubuntu features an open-world level design, with environmental worldbuilding inspired by anime influences such as the Sea Railway in Studio Ghibli’s Spirited Away and the desert landscapes of the game Journey. As a dialogue-free experience, Ubuntu presents an evolving environmental narrative through the core mechanic of world restoration where seeds sprout, trees grow, and the world gradually repopulates. The falling rain and the rippling trails left behind by the player serve as poetic metaphors for the ripple effects of solidarity and altruism through the Double It game mechanic.

NAMES ARE SACRED
​
​At the beginning of the game, the player is prompted to enter their name. In traditional multiplayer games, a username functions as a way to associate a player with their in-game presence, creating an immediate connection between the player and the experience. While most players might not dwell on the moment, instead focusing on gameplay, the simple act of entering a name sets the stage for something more meaningful.

​By the end of the game, the player is presented with a summary of their actions throughout the experience. In a narrative twist, the seemingly non-player characters they interacted with are revealed to be real players, as names and identities are assigned to previously faceless characters, fostering a sense of unexpected human-to-human connection.

THE SECRET WEAPON... LUDONARRATIVE DISSONANCE?

Ubuntu uses ludonarrative dissonance to challenge player perceptions and expectations of solidarity. Firstly, the use of player stand-ins lead to players being unaware that they are part of a shared, multiplayer world. Dissonance might arise when the gameplay, through the Take It mechanic, appears to reward selfishness by allowing players to prolong their survival in the world. In a bout of competitiveness, players might choose to Take It more often than they Double It, exploiting the system to maximise their personal restoration efforts.

However, by the end of the game, players will see that their selfish actions have been affecting the experiences of others and ultimately, their own as well. The more they take thoughtlessly from the system, the longer it will take for everyone to achieve the community goal. They will come to realise that only through a careful balance, and a commitment to cooperation, can they truly progress. After all, the reason they were able to sustain their survival throughout the gameplay in the first place was because previous players had chosen to Double It.

Through ludonarrative dissonance, it parallels the concept of altruism, where you may never know if your Double It actions trigger ripple effects of paying it forward in others, yet you continue to act with goodwill regardless. The revelation of the multiplayer world encourages players to play with a fresh perspective on how actions of solidarity affect the greater world.

retrospective

MINEs are a relatively unexplored and novel form of interactive storytelling. With this in mind, Ubuntu was a game design project where I set a personal goal to innovate within this emerging space. I started by researching the few MINEs available in the market to better understand their core ideas, strengths, and limitations, and how I could use those insights to create something distinct.

I experimented with various narrative techniques in pursuit of this goal. One technique that particularly fascinated me was ludonarrative dissonance, a concept that games often seek to minimise. Instead, I wondered: what if gameplay itself could embrace dissonance, using it to shift the player’s understanding of the narrative? This question led me to deliberately use ludonarrative dissonance as a tool to affirm the game’s moral theme of solidarity, turning the player's initial experience of dissonance into something meaningful.

The absence of dialogue introduced an additional dimension of challenge. Through the creation of environmental assets, I had to consider how to showcase an evolving world through the strategic placement of pixels on screen.

The final version is a proof of concept, limited by the timeframe of the assignment. While it isn't a fully-fledged multiplayer game, it was an ambitious project that pushed my ability to experiment and think critically about design. Preliminary feedback from players suggests that it succeeded in delivering a thoughtful and innovative experience.
